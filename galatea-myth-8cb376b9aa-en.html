Ovid · Metamorphoses X · 238–297

Pygmalion and the living statue

The complete story of an ivory figure, a cautious prayer, and the moment when art begins to breathe.

Read the story
Overview

The story in brief

After seeing the fate and conduct of the Propoetides, Pygmalion withdraws from women and lives alone. He carves a woman from ivory so lifelike that he falls in love with his own creation and cares for it as a beloved. At the festival of Venus he cautiously asks for a wife like his statue, and the goddess shows that she understands by making the altar flame leap three times. Back at home, the figure’s lips grow warm, the ivory softens, and a pulse begins to beat beneath Pygmalion’s hand. Venus attends their wedding; their daughter Paphos is later born, while the living woman remains unnamed in Ovid and receives the name Galatea only in later tradition.

I

Why Pygmalion lived alone

Immediately before Pygmalion's story, Ovid tells of the Propoetides of Cyprus. Because they deny the divinity of Venus, they gradually lose all sense of shame; in the end, the poem says, they harden into stone.

Pygmalion has seen their lives and is offended by their vices. He withdraws from women and marriage, lives unmarried for a long time, and has no partner in his bed. From that withdrawal, unexpectedly, his own story of transformation begins.

Ovid, Metamorphoses X, 238–246
II

The woman in ivory

With marvellous skill, Pygmalion carves snow-white ivory. He gives his work a beauty that, Ovid says, no woman could be born with. The figure looks so real that she seems alive and ready to move, held back only by modesty. The artistry conceals that it is art.

The sculptor admires his work—and falls in love with the body he has made. Again and again he touches it, testing whether it is flesh or ivory. He kisses the statue and imagines his kisses returned; he speaks to her, embraces her, and even fears that his fingers might leave bruises on her skin.

Ovid, Metamorphoses X, 247–258
III

Gifts for a beloved

He no longer treats the statue as an object. He brings her shells and smooth pebbles, little birds, flowers of a thousand colours, lilies, painted balls, and drops of amber. He dresses her, places gems on her fingers, long necklaces around her neck, pearls at her ears, and ribbons across her breast.

Yet even unclothed, Ovid says, she is no less beautiful. Pygmalion lays her on purple-dyed covers, calls her his bedfellow, and rests her neck on soft feathers—as though she could already feel their touch.

Ovid, Metamorphoses X, 259–269
IV

The festival of Venus

Then comes the festival of Venus, the goddess's most celebrated day throughout Cyprus. Heifers with gilded, curving horns are sacrificed; incense rises from the altars. After Pygmalion has performed his duty, he pauses before the altar.

His prayer is timid. If the gods can grant all things, he says, he wishes for a wife like his ivory maiden. He does not dare ask for the statue itself. Venus is present at her own festival and understands what he truly means: as a sign of her favour, the altar flame leaps up three times and sends its point into the air.

Ovid, Metamorphoses X, 270–279
V

The ivory grows warm

Pygmalion hurries home to his image. Bending over the couch, he kisses it: the lips seem warm. He kisses her again and tests her breast with his hand. Under his fingers the ivory loses its hardness; it yields and grows soft.

Ovid compares it to wax from Hymettus, softening in the sun and taking shape beneath a thumb. Pygmalion marvels, rejoices, and fears that he is being deceived. Again and again he touches what he longed for. Now it is flesh. Beneath his thumb the veins pulse.

Ovid, Metamorphoses X, 280–289
VI

The first gaze

Pygmalion now pours out his thanks to Venus. He presses his mouth to lips that are no longer false. The young woman feels the kisses, blushes, and timidly opens her eyes. At the same moment she sees the sky and her lover.

Venus attends the marriage she herself has brought about. After nine lunar months, the woman gives birth to a daughter: Paphos. With her name Ovid closes the episode and carries the story into the next generation.

Ovid, Metamorphoses X, 290–297

Galathée

When did she become Galatea?

Ovid gives the living woman no name. The familiar name belongs to the story's later reception. Jean-Jacques Rousseau's lyrical scene Pygmalion, written in 1762, explicitly calls her “Galathée”; the work was first performed in Lyon in 1770.

This establishes the essential point: the name “Galatea” does not come from Ovid's statue episode but is a later naming. In Rousseau's text, after awakening, Galathée touches herself, then a piece of marble, and finally Pygmalion—and speaks.

Rousseau, Pygmalion (written 1762; first performed 1770)
